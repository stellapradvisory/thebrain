I recently went on
NPR’s
The Indicator
to talk about the zero-click internet. I spoke with co-host Adrian Ma for about 40 minutes. The final episode was about 9 minutes, with maybe a few clips of my voice in it. So if you measured my appearance literally — by the seconds in which I was actually audible — you’d conclude I barely showed up.
But the episode opened with an example I’d given them: how old is Paul Rudd? You Google it, Google tells you, done. You don’t need to visit Wikipedia, or IMDb, or a People magazine article — unless you’re ready to pore over beauty secrets to Paul Rudd’s apparent immunity to aging. That little example ended up framing the whole episode. They used the stat that
68% of U.S. Google searches now end without a click
, and the rest of the story chewed on the same question we’d been kicking around: what happens when the platform just hands you the answer, and the website that actually did the work never gets the visit?
So, yeah. My literal quotes were brief. My ideas went further. And that’s what influence usually looks like — a lot smaller on the tape than it was in the room.
We love to measure the visible thing. The click, the quote, the referral, the nine minutes of airtime, the tracked conversion. It’s the part we can point at. But the part we can point at is almost always smaller than what actually happened. Someone reads your LinkedIn post and repeats your argument in a meeting you’ll never sit in. A reporter builds a story around your example and runs one sentence of the interview. Somebody hears your company on a podcast, files the name away, and searches for it three days later — showing up, helpfully, as “direct” traffic from nowhere. A buyer reads a genuinely good idea of yours, never clicks a single link, and still walks away understanding you better than they did that morning. The work did something. Your dashboard just can’t retrace its steps.
Which is a big reason I’ve gotten skeptical of treating attribution like a record of what worked. Attribution can tell you
what got
the credit. It can’t really tell you
what caused
the result — and sometimes it can’t even tell you where the thing came from.
A couple of years ago at SparkToro we ran an experiment
: we sent more than 1,100 visits across 11 social networks, then checked what Google Analytics had to say about it. A big chunk showed up as Direct, with no clue which platform actually sent it. It’s not that the traffic was untrackable. The platforms just weren’t telling us.
And even when you can see the activity perfectly well, the attributed number will happily walk you off a cliff.
Dropbox’s data science team ran month-long blackout experiments
on mobile advertising and search engine marketing. On paper the attributed return on ad spend looked great — 1.53 for mobile, around 2.0 for SEM. But the causal ROAS, the return that actually depended on the ads running at all, came back at 0.70 for mobile and 0.92 for SEM. Both underwater. As a result, Dropbox moved $25 million away from that low-incrementality spend, and its portfolio LTV:CAC improved by 53%. The attributed story and the causal story weren’t just a little different. They flat-out disagreed about whether the money was working.
Btw, I deep-dive into this attribution rant today on the Humans of Martech podcast.
Note that I’m railing against attribution specifically. I’m not mad at measurement. I love receipts. Given the choice I’d take an itemized one, ideally with pictures... but that’s not a thing. But be honest about what a receipt can actually prove. Sometimes it undersells the work — the way nine minutes of NPR undersold a 40-minute conversation. Sometimes it gives first prize to whichever activity happened to be easiest to see.
So look at your numbers with your eyes wide open. What’s missing from the picture? Who owns the data you’re leaning on? What would you genuinely
do differently
if the number moved? And when being wrong gets expensive — millions of dollars, a whole channel — can you actually test whether the activity caused the result, instead of just taking its word for it?
