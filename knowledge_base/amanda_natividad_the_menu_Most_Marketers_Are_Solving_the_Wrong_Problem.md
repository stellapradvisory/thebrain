Most marketers have been taught to solve for clicks.
How do we get more traffic?
How do we improve CTR?
How do we squeeze a little more efficiency out of the channel?
But in B2B especially, those are the questions that prevent us from seeing the bigger picture. Our job as marketers isn’t just to generate a click. Our job is to make our company more familiar, more trusted, and more likely to be chosen when the buyer is finally ready. Building trust at scale isn’t exactly the same as generating the click.
That’s part of why I’m so obsessed with zero click marketing. A lot of the marketing that actually moves people doesn’t happen in one neat, trackable moment. It happens gradually. Someone sees your point of view. They hear your name a few times. They start to understand what you do. They remember you later. Then, when the problem becomes urgent enough, you’re already on the shortlist. (Heck, there are still tons of people who don’t know that for the past several years, I’ve headed up marketing at B2B SaaS company
SparkToro
!)
This all doesn’t mean clicks don’t matter. They do. But too many marketers act like the click is the whole story, when it’s often just the visible part of a much larger, mostly invisible, process.
One thing
Brendan Hufford
said on today’s
Zero Click Marketing podcast
really stuck with me: before there was language for “zero click marketing,” he was already doing it. He stopped trying to win the obvious game and started building useful, standalone content in public. That body of work helped people get to know him, trust him, and eventually hire him.
Me, my Dr. Seuss t-shirt, and Brendan Hufford!
One way Brendan did this was through his “
100 Days of SEO
” project.
He committed to publishing every day. Not gated. Not overly polished. Just consistent, useful ideas shared in public.
That constraint did a few important things:
It forced volume.
You
don’t
can’t get stuck overthinking a single post when you know another one is due tomorrow.
It improved taste.
And when you publish frequently, you start to feel the difference between something that’s actually good and not just “good enough.” Practice is just the only way to get better at something.
It built memory.
Not because any single post went viral, but because people kept seeing his name attached to ideas that were worth paying attention to.
That’s zero click marketing in practice. Not one big swing. A body of work.
This is the part most marketers skip. They’ll agree with the idea — yes, we should build trust, yes, we should be more audience-first — but then they go right back to optimizing individual pieces of content for clicks. Nobody would ever disagree with building trust or creating content that resonates with our audiences. The thing, though, is that those are too hard to measure and there isn’t one prevailing way to do that.
If you want to actually try this, you don’t need a 100-day challenge — but you
do
need a constraint.
If I were doing this, here’s how I’d start:
Pick a lane.
What do you want to be known for? It can’t just be “marketing.” That’s not specific enough. If it’s growth marketing, ok… B2B or B2C? What sector? At what level? This one’s about the topic.
Define your differentiator.
What makes you uniquely qualified to do this? Why would people root for you? What are you already good at? What do you want to get better at? It could be that you’re a tenured operator who’s done B2B growth marketing for small businesses. Or maybe it’s that you’re a longtime content marketer who’s doing a slight pivot into paid marketing. This one’s about you.
Pick an audience of 3.
Think of someone you want to mentor, someone you look up to, and someone who’s your peer. This will (hopefully) help you focus on creating content that you think is genuinely useful. This one’s about your audience.
Commit to a cadence.
2–3x per week is (probably) enough. Daily if you’re ambitious. If it helps you to say you’re publishing something every Tuesday and Wednesday, go for it. But your audience doesn’t need you to deliver on a specific day/time — they need you to deliver regularly.
Make it Zero Click.
Each post should deliver standalone value without requiring a click. A post about a Meta ad campaign you ran shouldn’t require context about who you are and why you ran the campaign. But if people ought to follow you on Instagram (or YouTube? I don’t know, this is your content) it should be obvious where they should go to learn more from you, often and regularly.
And most importantly: Don’t measure this like a campaign. You’re not looking for immediate conversions. You’re looking for signals like, “I see you everywhere lately!” or “You’ve been on my list for months.”
Because by the time someone clicks, they’ve already made their decision.
That’s the shift I think more marketers need to make.
