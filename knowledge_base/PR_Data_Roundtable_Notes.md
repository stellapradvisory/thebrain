About this roundtable

This roundtable was hosted by CoverageBook in London as part of our wider ongoing

research into what is needed in the future of PR measurement and insights. For more

than a decade, we have focused on helping PR teams simplify reporting and save

time. As the industry evolves, we are now exploring how we can better support PR and

communications teams with deeper, more actionable insights from coverage and

measurement data not just reporting outputs.

The session brought together a mix of agency, in house, strategy, reputation, and

measurement professionals to discuss the realities of modern PR measurement: what

is working, where teams struggle, and what better systems could look like in practice.

Alongside the roundtable discussions, we have also been conducting broader

research with approximately 160 PR and communications professionals across

agency and in house teams in the UK and US. That wider research has surfaced

recurring themes around:

Not feeling confident in data led strategy or budget decisions

Fragmented measurement processes across teams and tools

Dependence on fragile spreadsheets and individual specialists

Concerns that measurement systems often collapse when key people leave

teams

The survey findings are included at the end of this document. The roundtable formed

part of this wider exploration into how the industry currently approaches measurement

and insight.

C O R E   T H E M E S

Eight themes from the discussion

1

How confident are teams in answering "what is
working?"

Confidence, capability & the knowledge gap

The discussion opened with a central question: when senior stakeholders ask "what is

actually working, and where should we invest more budget?" how confident are PR and

communications teams in the answer? The immediate reaction was sceptical.

One participant, who had worked in senior measurement roles and now advises

organisations independently, said many companies still do not have a strong or modern

comms measurement approach. Key themes included capability varying widely across

organisations, expertise sitting with individuals rather than teams, agencies not

necessarily being more advanced than clients, and measurement maturity remaining

inconsistent across the industry.

“

Not one brand I've spoken to would confidently answer that
question.

The group repeatedly returned to the idea that measurement often depends on one

person's knowledge, one spreadsheet, one specialist, or one isolated process strongly

reflecting CoverageBook's wider research findings that many teams fear their

measurement systems would collapse if a key person left.

"Measurement is often one person and a spreadsheet."

There was also discussion about accessibility. Several participants noted that comms

professionals are often drawn more to storytelling, creativity, persuasion, and stakeholder

management than spreadsheets or data architecture. This does not mean they do not

care about insight but systems need to make insight easier to access and harder to

avoid. One participant said measurement should become:

"Unavoidable."

2

Are we talking about budget allocation or budget
optimisation?

Two distinct measurement challenges

A useful distinction emerged between two separate measurement challenges: how much

PR budget gets allocated, and how PR uses its existing budget most effectively.

Participants noted these are related but fundamentally different problems.

"There's the question of how much of the pie PR gets and then there's the

question of how to use that pie better."

The conversation then shifted into a broader question: "Working to do what?" Participants

argued PR is often judged against short term commercial metrics, while its true

contribution is frequently longer term reputation building. PR was described as helping to

build trust, reduce barriers, strengthen resilience, attract customers and talent, and shape

long term perception.

Reputation is the invisible hand that makes business easier.

“

"You could stop PR tomorrow and maybe not feel it immediately. But you feel it

years later."

This led into a broader discussion about ROI language. Participants argued comms

teams often use "ROI" loosely when they actually mean efficiency, cost effectiveness,

proxy value, or comparative impact.

Key distinction: "ROI is a specific financial formula; comms often uses the

word too loosely." There was agreement that proxy metrics can still be useful,

but only when everyone understands what they represent and where their

limitations are.

3

How do you measure long term reputation inside
short term reporting cycles?

Activity vs. influence the reporting tension

One of the strongest themes was the tension between long term reputation building and

short term reporting expectations. Teams are often judged on daily coverage, reach,

share of voice, impressions, sentiment, weekly reports, and media wins. But participants

argued PR's real impact often develops over years, not weeks.

This led into repeated distinctions between proof of activity and proof of influence.

Hygiene reporting was described as the visible evidence that PR activity is happening

coverage volume, media hits, daily updates, campaign outputs.

It's proof of activity, not proof of influence.

“

The group repeatedly challenged reliance on coverage volume, impressions, estimated

views, vanity metrics, and numbers simply increasing year on year.

Coverage volume obsession

Impressions and estimated views

Vanity metrics without context

Numbers increasing year on year as a

proxy for success

"More coverage year on year isn't always better."

Participants argued stronger PR may sometimes mean fewer but more relevant pieces,

stronger message pull through, better audience alignment, or improved perception rather

than larger volume. Short term reporting still matters, but should not be mistaken for

strategic evaluation of PR's value.

Does PR control reputation or simply influence it?

4

Building evidence of influence over time

Participants noted that marketing can tightly control variables such as spend, audience,

creative, and placement. PR cannot control reputation in the same way. Reputation

depends on what people think, feel, say, and repeat. PR can influence those perceptions,

but not fully control them.

One participant summarised it simply:

You don't control reputation. You can influence it.

“

One measurement expert described the challenge by saying:

How do you eat an elephant? One bite at a time.

“

The point being that proving influence or reputation shift may never come from one

perfect metric, but instead from building layers of evidence, signals, and patterns over

time.

Participants stressed that short term reporting still matters as a hygiene factor. Teams still

need:

Coverage reports

Stakeholder updates

Campaign visibility

Proof that activity is happening

Short term reporting and visible outputs were seen as a permanent part of how PR teams

demonstrate activity and communicate progress internally. The discussion was less about

replacing reporting and more about adding smarter layers of analysis on top of it.

Participants were interested in better ways to understand:

Which messages are landing

How topics are being discussed

Which activity appears to be creating

Where teams should focus attention next

momentum

There was also discussion about whether stronger evidence from communications

activity could eventually be correlated against broader indicators such as:

Sales

Donations

Leads

Shifts in audience perception

But participants were careful not to overclaim direct causation.

This connected to a wider point that reputation differs by audience and market.

Participants stressed that measurement frameworks cannot simply be applied universally

without understanding:

Who the audience is

What perception matters

What the organisation is actually trying to

influence

5 Why is insight not shaping decisions fast enough?

From retrospective proof to real time optimisation

A major theme throughout the session was that measurement should help teams improve

work while campaigns are live not simply prove value afterwards. Participants wanted

measurement to answer: what should we do more of? What should we stop? Which

messages are landing? Which audiences are responding? Where should we shift

resource?

“

An obsession with measuring outcomes at the expense of
insights and incremental improvement.

The discussion repeatedly returned to the idea that organisations collect data but do not

spend enough time interpreting it, discussing it, connecting it to strategy, or changing

activity because of it.

"People don't want to sit with the insight."

There was also discussion about whether agencies proactively tell clients when

something is not working. Participants acknowledged this does not happen often enough

because of pressure to prove value, ego, client dynamics, fear of appearing ineffective,

and campaign reporting norms. The group agreed better measurement should reveal

what is not working early enough to change course.

Ego as a barrier: Teams competing for credit, agencies wanting to prove

success, leaders avoiding uncomfortable evidence, and reluctance to admit

something failed were all identified as recurring barriers to honest evaluation.

Participants emphasised that measurement is most valuable when it helps teams adapt

in real time shifting effort between channels, changing content formats, stopping

ineffective activity, investing more in successful messages, and reallocating resources

based on evidence. Participants warned that teams optimise for whatever they are

measured against, and poor metrics can encourage safer ideas, coverage for coverage's

sake, and prioritising visibility over strategic value.

Could AI unlock qualitative insight at scale?

6

The future opportunity for intelligent analysis

AI emerged repeatedly as one of the most important future opportunities. Participants

discussed using AI for analysing large volumes of coverage, detecting themes and

narratives, identifying belief shifts, analysing message pull through, testing messaging,

interrogating data faster, and supporting campaign optimisation.

“

Qualitative analysis at a scale we just couldn't do manually
before.

This connected closely to criticism of traditional sentiment analysis. Participants argued

positive/neutral/negative scoring is often too simplistic. Instead, they wanted systems that

better understand narratives, beliefs, audience language, strategic context, and whether

criticism may still be useful.

The group repeatedly discussed the need for more forward looking insight. Participants

argued leaders increasingly care less about retrospective proof decks and more about

predictive insight, early warnings, decision support, opportunity identification, and

strategic recommendations.

One strong product related theme was that users may not want to constantly inspect

dashboards. Instead, they want systems that proactively flag unusual changes, shifting

narratives, emerging issues, unexpected spikes, audience movement, and successful or

failing messages.

I shouldn't have to go looking for it.

“

7 What foundations are required for better

measurement?

Data architecture, consistency & shared systems

Participants repeatedly stressed the importance of strong data foundations. This included

clean data, consistent structures, shared systems, tagging frameworks, baseline

measures, and repeatable architecture.

"You can't interrogate trends if the structure underneath isn't consistent."

Without these foundations, deeper insight especially AI supported insight becomes

difficult. There was also a recurring concern that insight often does not reach the people

making tactical or strategic decisions. The people closest to the data are frequently not

the people running campaigns day to day.

Participants wanted insight placed directly into the hands of practitioners and account

teams so that learning and optimisation could happen continuously rather than

retrospectively.

8 Why does leadership buy in matter so much?

Culture, alignment & board level engagement

Participants said the best measurement cultures emerge when leadership actively

supports it. Senior stakeholders help define reputation goals, align teams, move beyond

vanity metrics, create common language, and connect comms to broader business

priorities.

“

The best work happens when the board is involved.

The discussion also touched on examples of leadership incentives being connected to

reputation or sentiment measures. Participants saw this as evidence that reputation is

increasingly being treated as materially important to organisations, not simply as a

communications concern.

Without leadership buy in, many teams felt they default back to reporting outputs

because those are the metrics senior stakeholders most visibly ask for.

Where this research is pointing

Across the roundtables, survey findings, and wider industry conversations, a theme is

starting to emerge. The industry does not need more reporting. It needs easier access to

consistent, actionable insight while activity is still happening.

The real value is not simply proving outcomes months later, but helping teams understand

what is working now, where momentum is building, what needs adjusting, and where effort or

budget should shift while campaigns are live.

We've taken one idea from the discussions in particular:

“A system of evidence for attempts at influence.”

This idea felt important.

A suggestion that emerged during the roundtable was that deeper analysis of coverage and

content could help provide evidence that communications activity is applying pressure in the

intended direction, even if that does not directly prove a real world change in audience

perception or business outcome.

That distinction matters.

PR teams are not always trying to claim direct control over reputation, behaviour, or

commercial outcomes. But they are trying to understand whether their work is contributing

meaningful influence over time and whether signals are moving in the right direction.

Participants repeatedly stressed that short term reporting still matters as a hygiene factor.

Teams still need visible reporting outputs, stakeholder updates, and proof that activity is

happening. The opportunity is not to replace reporting, but to layer smarter insight and

guidance on top of it.

Long term outcomes will always matter and they should flex depending on objectives,

audiences, and business priorities. What should stay consistent are meaningful signals and

strategic guidance throughout activity, not just retrospectively at the end of it.

Increasingly, PR teams are asking for insight that feels practical, accessible, and genuinely

useful in day to day decision making. Not heavier reporting processes or more complicated

dashboards, but structured insight that helps practitioners, strategists, and budget holders

make better decisions with greater confidence.

This is still an evolving area of research for CoverageBook, but the direction feels

increasingly clear. The future opportunity may not be more measurement for measurement's

sake, but making strategic insight easier to access, easier to act on, and more naturally

embedded into the flow of PR work itself.
