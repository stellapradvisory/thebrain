## Part 1: From Answer the Public to LLM Prompt Strategy

### The Starting Question

Stella's background includes involvement with **Answer the Public**, which she used to map audience search behaviour — branching out into longer-tail, situation-based, and seasonally influenced searches. The question she brought to this conversation was:

> *How much of that SEO audience research thinking applies to LLM prompts? Because prompts are already long-tail — but tools like Otterly are only giving me the obvious ones.*

### The Core Answer: Intent Survives, Interface Changes

The fundamental insight is that **the job is still the same** — understanding what people want to achieve, what questions they ask, what influences their decisions, what information they trust, and what language they naturally use. Answer the Public was uncovering those things. In SEO they were called keywords. In AI they are better thought of as **information needs or decision journeys**.

The difference is that traditional SEO targeted:
> *best SIM only deals*

AI users ask:
> *I'm moving abroad for six months. Should I keep my UK SIM or switch providers?*
> *My teenage son keeps running out of data. Which network has the best parental controls?*
> *I hate being locked into contracts. Which mobile provider is best if I travel a lot?*

These are not just longer keywords. **They are situations.** LLMs are brilliant at understanding situations.

### The Four-Layer Prompt Model

This conversation produced Stella's original formulation of the four dimensions of LLM prompts:

| Layer | What It Is | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Layer 1 — Topic** | The obvious subject | SIM only, contracts, roaming |
| **Layer 2 — Intent** | What the person is trying to do | Compare, buy, understand, troubleshoot, save money, reassure themselves |
| **Layer 3 — Situation** | The life context behind the prompt | "I'm taking my kids camping in France and need reliable data." |
| **Layer 4 — Conversation** | The follow-up dialogue that develops | Multi-turn conversation: "I'm travelling" → "What country?" → "Spain" → "How long?" → "I'll be working remotely" → "I use loads of hotspot" → "I don't want surprise charges" |

Traditional keyword research almost completely misses Layer 3 and Layer 4.

### Where Existing AI Visibility Tools Fall Short

Tools like Otterly, Profound, Goodie, and Peec AI mostly start from:
> *"What prompts mention my brand?"*

The better question is:
> *"What conversations should my brand naturally appear within?"*

That is much broader — and it is where the SEO audience research background becomes a genuine competitive advantage.

### The Audience Planning Analogy

The old SEO mapping was:
> Audience → Needs → Questions → Content → Measurement

The AI-native version should be:
> Audience → Life event → Decision → Prompt → AI citation → Business outcome

### giffgaff Prompt Cluster Examples

Rather than testing generic commercial prompts, Stella proposed building prompt clusters around audience situations for giffgaff:

**Money**
- "I'm trying to cut household bills."
- "My contract ends next month."
- "I hate hidden fees."
- "I don't want credit checks."

**Families**
- "My teenager needs their first phone."
- "My son keeps exceeding his data allowance."
- "We're trying to manage spending."

**Travel**
- "I'm travelling around Europe."
- "I'm moving overseas."
- "I work remotely."
- "I spend months abroad."

**Students**
- "I'm going to university."
- "I need a cheap plan."
- "I don't know how much data I use."

**Values**
- "I want an ethical company."
- "I hate being trapped by contracts."
- "I'd rather support companies with good customer service."

### The Strategic Distinction

> **Monitoring asks:** "Did we appear?"
>
> **Strategy asks:** Which conversations matter? Which life situations drive our category? Which prompts predict commercial intent? Which prompts influence consideration? Which ones does AI consistently answer well or poorly? Where should PR create authority so brands become the recommended answer?

This is where Stella's consultancy can be distinctive — thinking in **AI audience strategy**, not just AI visibility monitoring.

## Part 2: Measurement Architecture — Synthesising AMEC and Onclusive

### The Core Problem Being Solved

Stella's original question was whether AI visibility is an *outcome* of PR coverage or its own separate measurement environment. The answer is: **both, depending on the objective** — but they must never be collapsed into one score.

The most useful starting model is:

> PR activity → earned media evidence → machine discovery and retrieval → search/AI visibility → audience response → organisational impact

### The AMEC Three Connected Areas

AMEC proposes three areas that must be triangulated, not treated as a fixed sequence:

| Area | What It Covers |
| :--- | :--- |
| **A. Upstream Reputation** | Earned media, reviews, expert commentary, thought leadership, owned content |
| **B. Search & Content Readiness** | Findability, crawlability, indexation, credible links, citeable formats |
| **C. Downstream AI Outputs** | Presence, prominence, framing, source mix, message accuracy, errors |

AMEC explicitly says these three areas must be assessed together, not treated as a fixed sequence.

### Where Existing Coverage Analysis Sits

Stella's existing coverage classification belongs in **upstream reputation**. It does not need to be abandoned — it needs to be placed inside a wider system. The new question becomes:

> *Is the public record we are creating strong, credible, relevant, consistent and retrievable enough to support the way we want the brand to appear in AI-mediated discovery?*

### AI Output Analysis — The Parallel to Coverage Analysis

An AI answer is a different unit of analysis from an article, but similar dimensions apply:

| Earned-media analysis | AI-output analysis |
| :--- | :--- |
| Was the brand mentioned? | Did the brand appear? |
| Where was it mentioned? | How prominent was it in the answer? |
| Was the brand in the headline or opening? | Was it mentioned first, ranked highly or recommended? |
| What was the sentiment or framing? | How was the brand described or characterised? |
| Were priority messages included? | Were the intended messages and attributes reflected accurately? |
| What publication carried it? | Which sources were cited or linked? |
| How did competitors perform? | Which competitors appeared, ranked or were recommended instead? |
| Was the coverage accurate? | Did the answer contain errors, omissions or outdated claims? |

### Brand Prominence Exists at Every Layer

| Environment | What prominence means |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Earned media** | Headline mention, opening paragraph, image, quotation, message dominance |
| **Google search** | Ranking position, SERP feature presence, share of visible results |
| **LLM answer** | Early mention, recommendation status, depth of description, proportion of answer, position against competitors |
| **Audience response** | Recall, recognition, consideration, trust or preference |

### The Critical Distinction: Grounding, Citation, Mention, Recommendation, Referral

These are **separate events** and must never be conflated:

1. **Grounded** — information was retrieved or used behind the scenes
2. **Cited** — a source received visible attribution
3. **Mentioned** — the brand was named in the answer
4. **Recommended** — the answer actively selected or endorsed the brand
5. **Referred** — the user clicked through or visited a source

None automatically guarantees the next. This distinction protects Splendid from turning AI visibility into another vanity-metric service.

### AMEC Outputs → Out-takes → Outcomes → Impact

| Stage | What it covers |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Outputs** | Coverage, expert commentary, thought leadership, owned pages |
| **Out-takes** | Mentions, citations, prominence, framing, message accuracy, recommendations, competitive comparisons |
| **Outcomes** | AI referral sessions, engaged sessions, landing-page activity, enquiries, conversions, brand tracking |
| **Impact** | Sales, customer acquisition, reputation, recruitment, policy change, reduced risk |

AI visibility is normally best classified as an **out-take or intermediate result**, not automatically the final outcome. It can be an objective in its own right, but only when precisely defined.

### How to Connect Coverage with AI Visibility Without Overclaiming

Seven forms of evidence for a credible contribution case:

1. **Source correspondence** — Did earned-media domains secured by PR subsequently appear in AI citations?
2. **Narrative correspondence** — Did messages in coverage also appear in AI-generated brand descriptions?
3. **Temporal correspondence** — Did AI visibility shift after a period of relevant earned activity?
4. **Topic correspondence** — Did visibility improve specifically for the topics covered?
5. **Competitive correspondence** — Did the brand gain position where competitors previously dominated?
6. **Retrieval correspondence** — Did coverage become visible in search and then appear in AI citations?
7. **Behavioural correspondence** — Did cited pages receive identifiable AI referral traffic?

These do not prove that PR alone caused the change. Together they provide a much stronger contribution argument than a before-and-after visibility score.

### The Onclusive Four Diagnostic Gaps (useful for client workshops)

- **Presence gap:** the brand is absent from AI answers
- **Position gap:** competitors rank or are recommended more strongly
- **Narrative gap:** the brand is inaccurately or inconsistently described
- **Source gap:** influential sources do not support or sufficiently represent the brand

### Assessment of the Three Source Documents

| Document | Strength |
| :--- | :--- |
| **AMEC Practitioner's Guide** | Best high-level framework. Three connected evidence areas. Maps GEO into outputs, out-takes, outcomes and impact. |
| **AMEC Presentation** | Strongest source for measurement discipline. Clearly separates visibility from outcomes. Distinguishes grounding/citation/mention/recommendation/referral. Warns against treating small prompt samples as the market. |
| **Onclusive Playbook** | Most useful for practical metrics, client-facing diagnostics, and showing how AI source analysis can inform PR planning. However, its composite metrics and causal language need AMEC's caveats applied. |

## Part 3: The Framework Takes Shape

### The Separation That Makes the Framework Original

The key intellectual move was separating **what drives good PR** from **how well that PR performed**. These are different things.

This led to the clean seven-layer structure:

1. **Flexible Objectives** — the "why" (set per client and campaign)
2. **Fixed PR Drivers** — Activity, Coverage Type, Brand Prominence, Sentiment (diagnostic variables, not performance metrics)
3. **PR Coverage Performance** — Estimated Views, Engagements, Shares, Domain Authority, Links, Referral Traffic
4. **Brand Visibility Across Search & AI** — Search visibility, search rankings, AI visibility, AI citations, AI recommendations, brand representation
5. **Audience Outcomes** — Awareness, understanding, trust, consideration, preference, advocacy
6. **Behavioural Outcomes** — Search, visit, enquire, apply, buy, recommend
7. **Organisational Impact** — Revenue, growth, reputation, market share, recruitment, policy, risk reduction

### Why "Brand Visibility Across Search & AI" Is the Right Name for Layer 4

The conversation considered several names for Layer 4:
- Information Discovery
- Discovery & Representation
- Brand Discoverability
- Information Environment
- **Brand Visibility Across Search & AI** ← chosen

"Brand Visibility Across Search & AI" was selected because it is immediately understandable and clearly communicates that this layer measures how the brand exists within the ecosystem that people and AI systems draw upon — not the coverage itself.

The explanatory line for Layer 4:
> *How prominently, accurately and favourably the brand appears across search engines and AI-generated answers.*

### The Measurement Questions at Each Layer

| Layer | The Question Being Answered |
| :--- | :--- |
| Objectives | What are we trying to achieve? |
| PR Drivers | Did we create high-quality PR? |
| Coverage Performance | How well did that PR perform? |
| Brand Visibility Across Search & AI | How well is the brand being discovered and represented? |
| Audience Outcomes | What changed in the audience? |
| Behavioural Outcomes | What did people do? |
| Organisational Impact | What changed for the organisation? |

## Important Measurement Cautions

- There is no single stable measure of total LLM visibility
- No tool captures the complete market
- A tested prompt set is a sample, not the total audience
- Answers vary by platform, model, market, context and time
- Citation behaviour can change without notice
- A mention does not prove trust
- A citation does not prove a mention
- A recommendation does not prove behaviour
- A visibility change does not prove commercial impact
- AI products should not be merged into one aggregate score
- The testing methodology must be documented and reproducible

*These are not reasons to avoid measurement. They are what make the measurement credible.*

## The Earned-First Proposition

> **Not:** "Earned media gets brands mentioned by ChatGPT."
>
> **But:** "Earned-first strategy strengthens the trusted public information environment around a brand. We measure whether that evidence is authoritative, retrievable and accurately reflected across media, search and AI-assisted discovery — and whether it contributes to audience and business outcomes."
