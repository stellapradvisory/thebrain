I’ve been on the road these past several weeks — this week at
Experience Inbound
in Wisconsin which I highly recommend! — speaking on how to thrive in our zero-click world, and what the heck Zero Click Marketing actually is. I was preparing to do all this in a friendlier conversational format in a
Thursday webinar
with my friend
Matt Lerner of SYSTM
. While I was walking him through my deck, we had one of those productive disagreements that sticks with you.
I was explaining the deck like, “Here’s how I think marketing works now.”
Matt, who comes from more of a growth marketing background, saw it and basically said, “This is content marketing.”
The funny thing is, I don’t think either of us was wrong.
Enjoy this mostly out-of-context slide from my keynote. I just really wanted to add a visual for my open graph image and I don’t have anything else. But I think this probably makes sense on its own. Yes, there is a boat emoji there for no reason.
Because yes, the deck talks a lot about content. Native-to-platform posts. Search behavior. Public record. Reddit. AI answers. But to me, none of that is just “content.”
It’s how buyers understand you. Learn to trust you. Remember you. It’s how they decide you’re worth seeking out later.
That’s not a content problem. That’s a business problem.
For a long time, content marketing was treated like a traffic function. Publish the blog post. Rank for the keyword. Get the click. Capture the lead. Attribute the conversion. Repeat.
That system was never as clean as we pretended it was, but at least the story made sense.
Now the story is breaking.
Search keeps growing, but more searches end without a click. Social platforms punish links and reward native content. AI answers can shape perception before someone ever sees your homepage. Reddit threads, podcasts, creator mentions, comparison pages, and branded searches all influence the buyer journey in ways your analytics will never fully show.
So yes, content is involved.
But the job is bigger than publishing. The job is to create understanding, trust, recall, and preference wherever your buyer already is. That was one of the final ideas in my deck: the new job of digital marketing is not merely to send people to your website. It’s to build those four things across the places where decisions are actually shaped.
This is also why
Cyrus Shepard’s recent research
on Google winners and
Kevin Indig/AirOps’ research
on ChatGPT citations
felt so timely to me
.
Both point toward the same idea that broad content alone is becoming less defensible. The winners are more useful, more focused, more distinct, and harder to replace.
Cyrus’s research looks at which sites are winning in Google. His analysis suggests that the winners are more likely to offer a product or service, allow task completion, own proprietary assets, maintain topical focus, and have a strong brand.
Kevin and AirOps looked at ChatGPT citations and found something different but related: pages that closely match a user’s query seem to have an advantage over pages that try to cover everything.
Put those together, and the classic content marketing instinct starts to look shaky.
The answer is no longer to write the most comprehensive guide — it’s to build a destination worth seeking out that allows the user to fulfill their long-tail query or even better, their transaction.

…And this is where I think Matt’s reaction helped me see the tension more clearly.
A content marketer may look at Zero Click Marketing and see an argument about content distribution.
A growth marketer may look at it and see a debate about channels, conversion paths, and measurement.
A founder may look at it and see something else entirely: a question of whether the company is distinct enough to be remembered when the click never comes.
I think all of those readings are valid.
But I also think we’re reaching the point where they are converging.
The “content problem” and the “business problem” are becoming the same problem.
Because when clicks were abundant, you could get away with content that existed mostly to capture demand. You could write the “ultimate guide,” rank well, earn the click, and let the website do the rest.
But when clicks get scarcer, the content has to do more work before the visit ever happens. It has to explain the category, create trust, generate memory, and shape the public record. The content has to help Sales, Customer Success, Comms, Product, and Demand Gen do their jobs better. And if content has to do all this, then… it’s a function of business infrastructure.
This is the part I want marketers to take more seriously.
