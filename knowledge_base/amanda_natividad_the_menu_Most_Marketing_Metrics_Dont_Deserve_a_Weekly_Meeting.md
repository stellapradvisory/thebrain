Sure sure, everything should be measured. But I’m less interested in the minutiae of metrics and more interested in how that data changes your decisions, if it does at all. In other words:
What decision will this measurement help you make?
In
my recent conversation
with
Rachel Elsts Downey
, founder of podcast agency
Share Your Genius
, she explained that her company doesn’t obsess over every newsletter open, blog visit, or individual content conversion.
She looks at the bigger picture: whether the company’s audience is growing over time. More importantly, she looks at how many leads the business is generating, whether those leads are qualified, and whether they’re turning into the number of new customers the company needs to hit its revenue goals.
That’s it.
I’m not arguing against looking at newsletter performance. Open rates can reveal deliverability problems. Clicks can show which subjects interest readers. Replies obviously tell you a lot.
But before you spend hours inside a dashboard, ask what you’ll do differently because of what you find.
Would
one
weak newsletter convince you to abandon email? Probably not.
Would
one
low-performing LinkedIn post change your broader content strategy? I hope not.
Would learning that your team spends 20 hours a week producing a newsletter while qualified leads are falling short cause you to reconsider the investment? Yes. That information should change a real business decision.
The closer a metric gets to a meaningful decision, the more attention it deserves.
Rachel’s approach also creates room for experimentation. Her team knows the number of leads and customers the business needs. As long as marketing is helping produce them, the person responsible for marketing gets room to test ideas rather than endlessly optimize small things.
That freedom matters because each marketing channel requires more work than it appears to from the outside. A Pinterest account isn’t just a Pinterest account. It needs a strategy, expertise, production, distribution, and enough time to learn what works.
Trying to appear everywhere usually means doing mediocre work everywhere.
A better approach is to find the intersection between two things:
Where your audience already pays attention
Where you’re willing and able to create consistently
Then choose a channel. Build a repeatable system. Give it enough time and volume to teach you something. Only then consider adding another.
You don’t have to pursue every plausible marketing opportunity and monitor every available metric.
You need enough information to make good decisions about what to keep, what to improve, and what to stop doing.
That’s what measurement is for. We covered lots more in this week’s episode of the
