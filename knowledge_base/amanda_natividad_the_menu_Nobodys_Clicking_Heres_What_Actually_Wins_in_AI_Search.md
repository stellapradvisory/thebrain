People really don’t click anymore, ugh
From Kevin’s own studies and from outside work (he pointed to Pew): people are not clicking citations or sources inside AI answers. They’ll click a buy or purchase button. They mostly won’t click through to go read the underlying page.
The exception is what Kevin calls “high risk” prompts — medical, insurance, finance — where people do more fact-checking before they trust the AI’s answer at face value.
Ghost citations: the AI version of getting used but not credited
This was my favorite part of the conversation. A
ghost citation
is when your brand is cited as a source behind an AI answer but never actually named in the answer itself.
Kevin walked through the mechanics: an LLM retrieves content, decides what to cite, and separately decides what to mention by name — and those are three different filters, not one. In his research, only about 13% of brands were both cited and mentioned in the same answer.
The clearest example he gave was G2. Ask an AI “what’s the best CRM?” and it’ll pull heavily from G2 to form the answer — G2 gets ghost-cited constantly. But the answer itself will say “the best CRMs are HubSpot, Salesforce, etc.” — not “according to G2.” G2 gets used, not named. Reddit and LinkedIn work similarly: retrieved constantly, credited rarely.
If you’re a review site or an aggregator, that tradeoff is probably fine — it’s the business model. But if you’re a SaaS company or a DTC brand, Kevin’s point is blunt: citations barely matter because almost nobody clicks them. What matters is whether you get mentioned by name in the answer itself.
One more wrinkle: prompt length changes the behavior. Short prompts (”best CRM”) get more brand names thrown around because, absent greater, more specific context, the AI is trying to give you as many options as it thinks you need. Longer, more specific prompts narrow things down to fewer, more targeted mentions.
AI reads like a rushed editor, not a patient reader
Kevin’s data (drawn from analyzing over a million search results) points to AI systems reading content top-heavy — a lot of weight on the intro, a smaller bump of attention at the conclusion, and not much patience for anything else. His advice: write like classic journalism. Lead with the punchline. Structure it like a table of facts, not a story.
Here’s a great quote from him:
“If you wanna write for AI, it needs to be Wikipedia. Facts clearly structured, no storytelling, just all the most important information in the beginning.”
He used Malcolm Gladwell as the counterexample — a writer who deliberately withholds information to build toward a reveal. Great for humans. Terrible for AI, which wants the nugget up front, not a story arc. That zero-click tension — write for the machine that decides your visibility, or write for the human so they can enjoy reading it — is one neither of us has a clean answer for.
Trust is the currency now
This is the thesis Kevin kept coming back to, and the stat that backs it up is the one I keep repeating to people:
when Google shows an AI Overview at the top of results, clicks to Reddit and YouTube go up by roughly two-thirds.
Not because those sites are cited more (though YouTube increasingly is) — it’s because people read the AI’s answer, and then go looking for a human to confirm it actually sounds right.
