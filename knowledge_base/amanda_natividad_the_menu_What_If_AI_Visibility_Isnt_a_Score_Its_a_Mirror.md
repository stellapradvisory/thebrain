“What’s our AI visibility like?”
I think a lot more CEOs, CMOs, and founders are about to start asking this. And I get it. For 20 years, “how visible are we?” had an answer. Maybe not a great one, but an answer: rankings, organic traffic, branded search, share of voice, reviews, the analyst report someone paid too much for.
But buyers aren’t only searching anymore. They’re asking. They’re asking ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity for the shortlist, the comparison, the tutorial. So the executive question becomes: are we showing up there?
I think the better question is:
are we being described correctly?

This piece is sponsored by HubSpot, and I took them up on it because their
AEO Grader
gave me something concrete to poke at: my own company. Quick caveat before you screenshot me — I don’t think any AI visibility tool is gospel, and I’d gently suggest you not let a chatbot reorg your positioning. But marketers need a starting point, and a score is a fine place to start. It’s just not the whole story.
Here’s what I mean by mirror…
When I ran SparkToro through the grader, some of it was affirming. It pegged us as a niche audience research tool, picked up on our credibility with marketing practitioners, and understood that we help marketers figure out where their audience spends time — what they read, watch, listen to, and follow. Useful. Not because I needed the ego boost (though I’m human, compliments are nice), but because it confirmed the broad idea is landing. The public signals around us are strong enough that AI systems mostly get what we do.
Other parts needed a human read. We got lumped in with Semrush, Ahrefs, Similarweb, Brandwatch, and a few others. Some of those are fair-ish neighbors — if you’re researching a market or sizing up competitors, I can understand the overlap. But we’re not trying to be a broad SEO suite or an enterprise social listening platform. So the reflex is to say “the AI got it wrong.”
And maybe it did. But it’s more useful to ask why. Where did that comparison come from? Is the category itself confusing? Are buyers genuinely using those tools as mental substitutes for us? Is our public record (the third-party articles, review sites, comparison posts, podcast transcripts) sending mixed signals about where we belong?
This is where positioning becomes really important.
April Dunford
(you can’t talk about positioning and not mention April Dunford, this is marketing law) describes positioning as context-setting: it tells people what market you’re in, what you replace, who you’re for, and what they should compare you to. So if an AI tool drops you in the wrong competitive set, that might be an AI problem. But it might also be a *you* problem — the public internet may not have enough clear, consistent evidence about where you actually belong.
Which means you have to resist two instincts, both wrong.
The first is taking the output as truth. “Gemini says we’re in this category, so I guess that’s the category.” No.
The second is waving it off. “That’s dumb, the AI is stupid, moving on.” Also no.
Because even when the output is wrong, the wrongness is information. If AI systems believe something off about you, there’s a decent chance some slice of your market believes it too.
So when I read one of these AI visibility reports, I’m not just asking whether we’re visible; I’m asking whether we’re understood.
Here’s the practical version. If your CEO asks about your AI visibility, don’t hand them a number and call it done. Run the grader, then sit with three questions:
What’s accurate? Those are the signals worth reinforcing.
What’s wrong or incomplete? That’s where you’re getting miscategorized, or where the nuance is falling out.
And what does all of it say about your public record? Maybe you need clearer website copy, a better comparison page, sharper case studies, more third-party proof.
Then pick a short list. Nobody will actually work through your 47-item AEO roadmap, and they’ll resent you for building it. Pick the handful that matter: one product page, one comparison page, one customer story, one partner description. Fix it. Then get the idea off your own site. Talk about it on a podcast, put it in a newsletter, post it on LinkedIn, work it into a webinar.
