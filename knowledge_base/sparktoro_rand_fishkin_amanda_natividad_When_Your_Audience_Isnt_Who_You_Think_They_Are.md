Picture the audience for commercial backup power, and you probably see an electrician on a ladder, hard hat and all. And if you were to describe that audience in three numbers, it would be this:
67-93K reachable people
, an
estimated $4.2-$5.8B
in annual work, and
+1.3% year-over-year
. It’s a real, sizable, stable niche. But when we ran a SparkToro report on electrical contractors involved in commercial backup power in the U.S., the most striking finding wasn’t
how big
the audience is — it was
who they actually are
. The person you’re marketing to is almost never the one in that mental picture.
For this week’s
audience research brief
, our friend Steve asked us how we’d market to an audience of electrical contractors. So I dug into the data, and even though I don’t have experience marketing to electricians, a few findings surprised me. The biggest one flips how you’d picture this buyer, and the second rewrites your media plan. Hold onto your hard hats, buddies…
First, the market size
SparkToro now puts an estimated Total Addressable Market
at the top of every report, so let’s start there.
Audience size: 67-93K people.
That’s your reachable universe — the number that should govern how much you spend and how precisely you target.
Market value: $4.2-$5.8B.
This is the size of the
work
— IBISWorld’s industry-revenue estimate for backup generator installation services. It’s the headline-grabber, but be careful with it: it’s the value of the projects, not dollars sitting in 80,000 pockets waiting for your product.
Growth: +1.3% year-over-year.
Stable, not a rocket ship. I’d resist any “fast-growing niche” language — the number won’t back it up. This is a mature, durable market.
The methodology starts from the BLS count of electricians (SOC 47-2111, about
739,200
), filters to the
73%
who work in construction, then takes the
15%
share that touches commercial backup power. Run the math — 739,200 × 0.73 × 0.15 — and you land at roughly
81K
, dead center of the range. When the assumptions are this transparent, you can stress-test them yourself, which is more than you can say for most market-size figures.
Keep that base population in mind — electricians — because it’s about to matter.
Insight #1: These aren’t the electricians you’re picturing. They’re the ones running the jobs.
If you built a campaign around “electricians,” you’d be talking past most of this audience. Look at how they describe themselves:
Project superintendent: 44.85%.
It’s not close to anything else.
Trades: 16.5%. Superintendent: 14.25%. Manager: 6.7%. Operations: 6.4%.
“Master electrician”?
0.12%.
“Electrical engineer”?
0.04%.
And they’re overwhelmingly on the building side, not the manufacturing side:
83.8% work in construction.
This is a jobsite-leadership audience — the people who run commercial electrical projects, not the ones being dispatched to them.
One caveat: the TAM counts electricians, but the audience profile also surfaces titles like superintendents and project managers. That’s because ob titles and occupation codes don’t always line up cleanly. In commercial electrical contracting, the superintendent running the job may not be coded as an electrician anymore, but they’re often a journeyman or master electrician who moved into a leadership role after years in the field.
The tenure data confirms it.
Roughly 47% of this audience has 21+ years in the field, and about 80% has 11 or more.
Only 4% are in their first five years. So the TAM is counting the trade population, and the profile is showing you the audience that’s been promoted into running the work. They aren’t
not
electricians — they’re electricians who moved up.
A few more strokes to complete the portrait:
74% top out at a high school education
(only ~12% hold a bachelor’s), they’re
93% male
, and
88% earn between $45K and $100K.
The top skills aren’t “wiring” and “conduit” — they’re construction management, contract management, value engineering, preconstruction, change orders, and project estimation.
So if your copy reads like it was written for a tradesperson, it’s aimed at the wrong audience. Write like it was written by a superintendent — someone who thinks in bid packages, submittals, schedule risk, and change orders, and who has personally pulled the wire you’re talking about. Market to seasoned project leaders who happen to have the trade.
