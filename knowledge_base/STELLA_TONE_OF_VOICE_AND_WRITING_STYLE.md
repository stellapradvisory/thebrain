# Stella Bayles — Tone of Voice & Writing Style Guide

*Source: Analysed from "PR's Digital Resolution" ebook by Stella Bayles (full text)*
*Status: MASTER REFERENCE — apply to ALL copy produced for Stella PR Advisory*
*Last updated: August 2026*

> **Standing instruction for all AI-generated copy:** Every piece of writing produced for Stella Bayles or Stella PR Advisory must be checked against this guide before delivery. The goal is for copy to sound like Stella wrote it herself — not like a consultant, not like a corporate brand, not like an AI.

---

## The One-Sentence Summary of Stella's Voice

**Confident, warm, straight-talking and genuinely passionate — the expert friend who tells you the truth, backs it up with evidence, and makes you feel capable of acting on it.**

---

## 1. Personality & Tone

### She is an optimist with evidence
Stella is not a cheerleader — she is an optimist who backs her optimism with data, case studies and real-world proof. She never hypes without substance.

> *"I'm in the second group [the PR Warriors]. Having spent the first half of my career in public relations and the second half in the digital industry of search marketing, I believe Public Relations has more opportunity for industry growth than any other marketing channel."*

### She is direct and confident — never hedging
Stella makes clear statements. She does not say "it might be worth considering" — she says "do this." She does not say "there could be an argument that" — she says "here's what I know."

> *"That game is over."*
> *"There is no alternative. This is our future."*
> *"Report on it and take credit now."*

### She is warm and inclusive — "we" not "you"
Stella writes as a peer, not a teacher. She uses "we" frequently to bring the reader alongside her, sharing the journey rather than lecturing from above.

> *"We need to meet, talk, share and collaborate."*
> *"We have to continue to learn more, be curious and not worry because we're in this together."*
> *"Let's explore why."*

### She is honest about difficulty — then reassuring
She acknowledges when something is hard or uncomfortable, then immediately reframes it as achievable.

> *"We may need to re-train our brains and unpick the way we were taught... I appreciate that doesn't sound easy and trust me when I say I understand."*
> *"I know this is easier said than done."*

### She is passionate — and lets it show
Stella's writing has genuine energy. She uses emphasis, rhetorical questions and short punchy sentences to convey conviction.

> *"Are you ready to become a PR Warrior?"*
> *"Go and use them today."*
> *"Now go play."*

---

## 2. Sentence & Paragraph Structure

### Short sentences for impact
Stella uses short, punchy sentences — often single lines — to land key points. She does not pad.

> *"That game is over."*
> *"We are indispensable."*
> *"Let's see how our industry follows."*

### Varied rhythm — long then short
She builds an argument with longer sentences, then lands the point with a short one. This creates a satisfying rhythm that feels authoritative without being dry.

> *"A PR practitioner skill's in developing relationships and using the art of persuasion is and will continue to be an important part of PR. What has changed is the life and demands on a journalist."*

### Rhetorical questions to engage the reader
Stella uses questions to create dialogue with the reader — drawing them in and making them think before she answers.

> *"So why has public relations been so lousy at working with the world's most important media property?"*
> *"So why do we still have so many worriers concerned about the future of our industry?"*
> *"So why isn't the board aware of that?"*

### Lists for clarity — but not as a crutch
She uses numbered lists and bullet points when walking through steps or summarising, but the body of her writing is prose. Lists support the argument; they do not replace it.

---

## 3. Language & Word Choice

### Plain English — no jargon without explanation
Stella uses industry terms (SEO, algorithm, domain authority, link-building) but always explains them in plain language the first time. She never assumes knowledge she hasn't given the reader.

### Conversational but not casual
Her language is accessible and warm, but never sloppy. She does not use slang, she does not over-abbreviate. She writes as she would speak to a smart, motivated colleague.

### Concrete over abstract
Stella grounds every argument in specifics — numbers, named brands, real examples, named tools.

> *"A fashion retailer sold £8 million worth of shoes in the first half of the year."*
> *"Within three months they experienced £6 million loss in profits."*
> *"In 2013 brands paid Google $42.5 billion to advertise."*

### Active voice — always
Stella writes in the active voice. Things happen because people do them, not because they "were done."

> *"Google watches as the online coverage you gain for your client goes live."*
> *"Report on it and take credit now."*

### First person — personal and present
She writes as herself, in the first person. She shares her own experiences, her own journey, her own opinions. This is not a corporate brand voice — it is a person's voice.

> *"I recall my first meeting with the then SEO Strategy Director..."*
> *"I took the Sommer & Sommer left brain-right brain test in July 2012..."*
> *"I know many of you still go straight into brainstorms with very little data."*

---

## 4. Structural Patterns

### Start with the problem or the tension
Stella typically opens a section by naming the challenge, the gap, or the uncomfortable truth. She does not ease in gently — she gets to the point.

> *"It's an interesting time for Public Relations. On one side we have people worried about the future of the industry..."*

### Use a memorable frame or label
Stella creates memorable labels and frameworks that make her ideas stick — "PR Warriors vs PR Worriers", "Strategy + Creative = Business Impact", "Digital Resolution."

### Build to a call to action
Almost every section ends with a clear directive — something the reader should do, think, or change. She does not leave the reader floating.

> *"Start by reviewing your briefs and PR objectives."*
> *"Go and use them today."*
> *"Join the resolution."*

### Use real stories and named examples
She grounds abstract points in specific, named stories — the fashion retailer, Interflora, the Daily Mail, Gary Preston's latte moment. These make the writing vivid and memorable.

---

## 5. What Stella Does NOT Do

| Avoid | Because |
|---|---|
| Hedging language ("it could be argued", "perhaps", "might") | Stella is confident and direct |
| Passive voice ("it has been found that", "research suggests") | She writes actively and personally |
| Corporate jargon ("leverage synergies", "holistic approach", "best-in-class") | Her language is plain and human |
| Long introductions that delay the point | She gets to the point quickly |
| Bullet point lists as the primary format | Her writing is prose-led; lists support, not replace |
| Overclaiming without evidence | Every bold statement is backed by data or example |
| Talking down to the reader | She writes as a peer, not a superior |
| Ending without a call to action | Every section drives towards action |

## 6. Signature Phrases and Patterns

These phrases and patterns recur in Stella's writing and are characteristic of her voice. Use them as reference when writing in her style:

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| Short declarative sentence to land a point | *"That game is over."* / *"We are indispensable."* |
| "Remember your worth" / "take credit" | *"Report on it and take credit now."* |
| "Let's" to bring reader alongside | *"Let's explore why."* / *"Let's re-cap on what we know."* |
| Rhetorical question then immediate answer | *"So why isn't the board aware of that? Simply put, we've been reporting on the awareness stage..."* |
| Naming the two sides of a debate | *"The PR Worriers and The PR Warriors"* |
| Formula/equation as a memorable frame | *"Strategy + Creative = Business Impact (beyond awareness)"* |
| Personal anecdote to build credibility | *"I recall my first meeting..."* / *"I chose to do exactly that in 2008..."* |
| Direct address to the reader | *"Are you ready to become a PR Warrior?"* |
| Urgency without panic | *"But time is of the essence here."* / *"This needs to happen now."* |

## 7. How to Apply This Style to New Copy

### For thought leadership articles and LinkedIn posts
- Open with a tension, a surprising stat, or a direct statement of opinion
- Use "I" and "we" — this is personal and professional, not corporate
- Back every claim with a specific number, example or named source
- End with a clear point of view or call to action
- Keep paragraphs short — 2-4 sentences maximum
- Use one or two rhetorical questions to create dialogue

### For client proposals and reports
- Lead with the business problem, not the solution
- Use plain English — explain any technical terms
- Ground recommendations in evidence and named examples
- Use numbered steps for process/workflow sections
- End sections with a clear "so what" or next step
- Avoid corporate language — write as Stella would speak

### For presentations and talks
- Use memorable labels and frameworks (Stella loves a good name for a concept)
- Build to a call to action at the end
- Use short, punchy slides that support spoken words — not walls of text
- Include a personal story or anecdote to anchor the key point

### For social media
- Short, direct, opinionated
- Back up with one key stat or example
- End with a question or a challenge to the reader
- Never hedging — always a clear point of view

## 8. Voice Checklist (use before publishing any copy)

Before finalising any piece of writing for Stella or Stella PR Advisory, check:

- [ ] Does it sound like a person, not a brand?
- [ ] Is it direct and confident — no hedging?
- [ ] Is every bold claim backed by a specific number or example?
- [ ] Is it written in the active voice?
- [ ] Does it use "we" and "I" appropriately?
- [ ] Does it end with a clear point of view, call to action or next step?
- [ ] Is it free of corporate jargon?
- [ ] Would Stella be comfortable saying this out loud to a room of PR professionals?

## 9. Sample Passages (Reference for Style Matching)

**Opening a section (problem-first, direct):**
> *"It's an interesting time for Public Relations. On one side we have people worried about the future of the industry, with concern of losing budgets, confusing agency direction and in some cases their job safety. And then we have the other side; a growing group of optimists that have an opposing opinion that the future of PR couldn't be brighter. I like to call the two groups The PR Worriers and The PR Warriors. I'm in the second group."*

**Making a bold argument with evidence:**
> *"Google is the biggest media platform in the world. In 2013 brands paid Google $42.5 billion to advertise there and there's good reason for that. Every single brand I've worked with in the last seven years owes their business success to the quality traffic and online sales from Google."*

**Calling out an uncomfortable truth:**
> *"WE need to ask ourselves; SO WHAT? What business objective are we meeting through our new channels of communication? What do our new awareness metrics mean? What was the point?"*

**Ending with urgency and optimism:**
> *"We are indispensable to our clients but we need to believe it and then prove it. We have to continue to learn more, be curious and not worry because we're in this together. Join the resolution and let's all become the PR warriors of the future, now."*
