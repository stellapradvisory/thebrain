I’ll admit I’m kind of a thinkboi (gender neutral).
I’m often more interested in the philosophies behind marketing — how we choose our campaigns, which ideas are worth exploring, what deserves more of our attention — than in the campaigns themselves.
So when
Tim Soulo
asked me a question on
the Ahrefs podcast
that I couldn’t immediately answer, I did what any thinkboi would do: I’ve been turning it over ever since.
The question was:
If you can’t justify spending money to promote your content, how did you justify spending the time and effort to create it in the first place?
I mean, if an idea is valuable enough to research, write, edit, publish, and distribute organically, shouldn’t it also be valuable enough to advertise?
Maybe. But I don’t think creating and promoting are the same decision.
You create and build upon an idea because it deserves to exist. You pay to promote it because you decide a specific audience, channel, and campaign are the best use of a finite budget right now.
An idea can be worth making without necessarily being an efficient advertisement. Some ideas hit immediately. Others travel slowly through blog posts, podcasts, webinars, presentations, conversations, and social posts before becoming part of how people understand a subject.
Still, “the idea matters” can become a convenient excuse for publishing and hoping. So the better question may be:
What kind of support does this idea deserve?
Sometimes the answer is paid media. Sometimes it’s a webinar, podcast tour, keynote,
a book (cough cough)
, or a permanent home on your website.
The obligation isn’t to advertise every idea you believe in. It’s to give the best ones more than one chance to matter—and to be honest about whether you’re actually doing that, or just hoping.

Ahrefs podcast
to debate Zero Click Marketing, content distribution, attribution, personal reputation, and whether three great posts really beat five mediocre ones.
Despite the confrontational episode title, this was a generous, thoughtful conversation — and Tim asked questions that forced me to examine some of my own assumptions.

2. Every marketing yes contains a no
On my own podcast, I explored one of the defining skills of modern marketing: deciding what
not
to do.
Every time you choose a campaign, channel, optimization project, or new initiative, you are also choosing what will receive less time, money, and attention. The episode is only 8 minutes, because I respect both opportunity cost and your podcast queue.
» Listen: Marketing Is Choosing What Not to Do
3. Make what people want. Let your values lead.
I also joined the
Do Less Marketing
podcast to talk about building an audience, making work people actually want, and letting your values shape how you grow.
We discussed audience research, useful content, reputation, and the tension between doing what performs and doing the kind of work you believe should exist.
