Executive Summary

The Coverage Type launch should be framed as the next step in CoverageBook’s broader 

classiﬁcation and coverage analysis story: helping PR teams move from simply collecting 

coverage into understanding what kind of coverage they earned, why it matters, and 

how to explain it clearly to clients or stakeholders. CoverageBook already positions itself 

as the faster, cleaner alternative to manual clipping, scattered spreadsheets, and 

overcomplicated dashboards, with a workﬂow built around adding coverage, receiving 

screenshots and credible PR metrics, then sharing reports online, as PDFs, or via CSV 
exports. 1  The Coverage Type feature should build directly on that promise by adding a 

simple, consistent classiﬁcation layer to each item of coverage.

The recommended core message is: “Show not just how much coverage you earned, 
but what kind of coverage is driving the story.” This keeps the launch aligned with 

CoverageBook’s existing emphasis on credible, realistic, easy-to-understand PR 
measurement rather than positioning the feature as another abstract data ﬁeld. 2  It also 

connects naturally to the classiﬁcation project’s wider messaging around better analysis, 
prominence, cleaner datasets, and making PR reporting more useful for both human 

readers and future AI-assisted evaluation.

What’s Shipping?

Coverage Type introduces a more structured way to identify and analyse the role or nature 

of each piece of coverage inside CoverageBook. Instead of treating coverage only as a URL, 
platform, screenshot, clipping, or channel, the product can now recognise or let users label 
the type of coverage they have earned. This gives teams a clearer view of whether their 
work is producing, for example, news mentions, reviews, interviews, features, thought 
leadership, product coverage, campaign coverage, or other strategically meaningful forms 

of PR output, depending on the ﬁnal taxonomy used in-product.

This matters because CoverageBook already supports a wide range of media formats, 
including online articles, YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, X, Spotify, Twitch, Facebook, 
LinkedIn, print, and broadcast. 3  However, media format is not the same as coverage 
type. A print clipping, online article, or LinkedIn post tells the user where coverage 

appeared. Coverage Type helps explain what the coverage actually is and therefore how it 

should be evaluated.

meaningful label for what kind of PR result it represents. It is not just “this is an online

category. That classiﬁcation makes reports, exports, and analysis easier to interpret.

Why Does It Matter?

PR teams are under increasing pressure to demonstrate the value and quality of their work, 

not just the quantity of coverage they secured. CoverageBook’s current positioning 

already leans into this need by promising credible PR metrics and rejecting inﬂated, 

unrealistic measures such as AVE. 1  Its “Why us?” page contrasts outdated reporting 

habits, messy dashboards, and spreadsheet-heavy workﬂows with realistic metrics, 

consistent data, and reports that are easy for clients and stakeholders to understand. 4

Coverage Type solves the next problem in that journey. Once a team has gathered coverage 

and credible metrics, the next question becomes: what did this coverage actually do for 

us? A hundred coverage items can mean very diﬀerent things depending on whether they 

are passing mentions, detailed features, expert commentary, product reviews, interviews, 

backlinks from high-authority publishers, or social ampliﬁcation. Without classiﬁcation, 

users may still have to read every clip and manually explain the coverage mix.

This matters right now because CoverageBook’s own product language increasingly 

points toward clean, structured coverage data as the foundation for better human and AI-

assisted analysis. CoverageBook says it helps structure reports and data consistently so 

they can be shared with AI models and agents for evaluation, analysis, and insights, while 
warning that irrelevant or messy inputs lead to unreliable outputs. 1  Coverage Type 
strengthens that “clean source of truth” story. It gives PR teams a practical classiﬁcation 
ﬁeld that makes the dataset more meaningful before any deeper analysis happens.

Who Is It For?

The primary audience is existing CoverageBook users who already create reports 
regularly and want to make the interpretation of those reports sharper, faster, and 

more defensible. The feature has the strongest immediate value for agencies because 
agencies need to explain results to clients, prove strategic value, and compare coverage 

across campaigns or retainers. It also has strong value for in-house communications teams 

that need to brief leadership, benchmark coverage quality over time, and turn earned 
media into clearer business-facing narratives.

What Are They Doing Instead Today, and Why Is It Not Good Enough?

Today, users are likely solving this in one of four ways. Some are using comments or 

custom ﬁelds to describe the role of a coverage item. Some are maintaining a separate 
spreadsheet with manual categories. Some are relying on folder or report naming 

conventions to imply coverage type. Others are simply leaving it to the report reader to 
infer the type of coverage from the title, screenshot, or outlet.

These workarounds are not good enough because they are inconsistent, hard to analyse, 

and hard to repeat across teams. A comment is useful for human explanation, but it is not a 
reliable classiﬁcation system. A spreadsheet can be ﬂexible, but it duplicates work and 

separates insight from the report. Naming conventions are fragile. Reader inference creates 
ambiguity. CoverageBook’s existing positioning is already that teams need a clean list of 

actual coverage and a consistent source of truth rather than a messy tracker. 1  Coverage 
Type extends that logic from “what coverage did we earn?” to “what kind of coverage 

did we earn?”

their reporting workﬂow, not simply whether they see the launch announcement. The 
strongest behavioural indicator is the percentage of eligible coverage items with a 
Coverage Type assigned within active reports and Coverage Vault workﬂows. A secondary 

signal is whether users use Coverage Type in exports, ﬁlters, summaries, or analysis-related 
actions, depending on what is available in the product at launch.

Final Recommendation

Launch Coverage Type as a measurement maturity feature, not an admin feature. The 
strongest story is that CoverageBook is helping PR teams move from coverage collection to 
coverage understanding. This sits naturally inside the wider classiﬁcation launch because it 
teaches users a simple but powerful idea: better PR analysis starts with better-structured 

coverage data.

The most important messaging choice is to avoid making users feel they are being asked to 
do extra categorisation work for CoverageBook’s beneﬁt. Instead, frame Coverage Type as 
a way to make their own work easier to explain. The feature should be introduced as a 
practical reporting advantage: less manual interpretation, fewer spreadsheet 
workarounds, clearer client and stakeholder conversations, and cleaner data for 
future analysis.
